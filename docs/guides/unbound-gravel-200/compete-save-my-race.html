1. Decide whether this plan fits

This is a high-volume emergency build for a rider who already tolerates long consecutive training days and has a tested bike, nutrition system, and recent gravel experience. It is not an entry plan or a way to create a 200-mile endurance base in six weeks. If you cannot comfortably handle the first week, choose a longer runway or reduce your race goal with a coach.

Read the dose before starting. The delivered calendar totals about 16:20, 18:07, 21:07, 13:26, 17:42, and 17:49 by week, including its placed race-day card in week six. Its third week exceeds the old 12–18-hour claim. Do not add hours to hit a range. The 21-hour peak and the final-week race card make this a demanding product; use the actual calendar and your current capacity to decide.

Set FTP from your current testing, then use power targets as a guide alongside terrain and effort. If testing is unsafe or you are already exhausted, do not force a maximal assessment to make a calendar cell turn green. In TrainingPeaks, leave useful notes on readiness, execution, and nutrition after key rides. A missed session does not create a debt to repay by stacking hard rides.

3. How to ride the sessions

Power and the way you produce it

Check each Bike workout's first power step before you start. If a hard interval appears first, begin with easy Z1–Z2 riding and a gradual ramp until you are ready for that work. Do not launch straight into a hard target because the device starts there. On endurance rides, spend most time in the lower to middle part of your prescribed easy range. Ride the hoods for control and breath, then use short, timed spells in the drops on safe straight sections to learn how your back, shoulders and breathing respond. Return to the hoods before rough terrain or traffic demands it.

Use cadence as a skill variable, not an excuse to force power. On suitable flat or mild grades, alternate your normal cadence with brief higher-cadence spins while keeping the same easy power. For low-cadence torque work, stay seated, pedal smoothly, and stop the set if knee pain appears; choose a comfortable cadence and discuss persistent symptoms with a clinician or coach. Low cadence is optional where the surface makes traction or control unsafe.

Gravel specificity

Use the long rides to practice picking a line, relaxing your hands, braking before loose corners, and fueling while the road is uneven. A hard surge into a climb should be followed by deliberate recovery, not a long overshoot because the group went away. In the week-three and week-five simulations, rehearse your own opening pace, position changes, tire pressure, repair kit, and feed handoffs. Record what worked and change one variable at the next rehearsal.

After every key ride, answer three questions in your workout comments: What went well and why? What went badly and why? What is one small action within your control for the next ride?

4. Fuel and hydrate during training

Arrive fueled. Eat a normal carbohydrate-containing meal before long or hard rides, and bring enough food to start fueling early. Do not deliberately ride fasted. If a placed workout mentions fasting, follow this newer guidance instead and flag that wording for correction. Practice the amount and forms you can use while riding on rough roads; write down hourly intake and any gut symptoms after each simulation.

Higher output, intake history and tolerance matter. Do not infer that more sugar always makes a faster race. Your final race strategy needs your expected duration and the current event support map.

Start hydrated, then adjust bottle volume to your own sweat loss, weather and available resupply. Record what you drank and how you felt on long rides. Sodium needs vary: a rider with little salt residue may start near 500 mg per bottle, visible salt near 750–1,000 mg, and a very salty sweater may need around 1,500 mg. Bottle size, drink concentration, conditions and clinician advice matter; do not treat those examples as universal hourly prescriptions. Avoid trying a new concentrated mix on race day.

5. Strength delivery is missing

This plan currently includes no scheduled strength sessions. Any older guide promise of a built-in or periodized strength program was inaccurate. Follow the Bike calendar as delivered; do not assume a strength prescription is hidden in it.

If you already have a familiar strength routine, discuss whether a reduced maintenance dose fits your recovery; do not insert new maximal lifts or jumps next to the plan's hard rides. If strength is new to you, this six-week high-volume build is a poor time to start a demanding program. 6. Race intelligence and strategy

The organizer lists the 2027 UNBOUND 200 for 5 June in Emporia, Kansas. Its athlete-guide page points to the 2026 guide as of 3 October 2026; the route page likewise shows 2026 routes. Before travel, download the guide and route for your edition. Do not rely on an older checkpoint mileage, aid promise, elevation number or cutoff time.

Organizer rules to prepare for now. The official rules require each rider to have a person available to collect them if they abandon; riders may share that person or arrange Crew For Hire. Carry a navigation system with the current route because roads are open to traffic and markings alone are insufficient. Bring the required helmet, phone, nutrition and fluid capacity, and attend rider sign-in before race day with photo ID. Support and feeding are limited by category and designated zones: age-group checkpoints differ from elite feed zones, and some stops provide water without crew access. In the 200-mile event, drafting across race categories is restricted. Confirm your category's exact current rules before choosing a group or support plan.

Make the long day executable

  1. Start contained. Ride the first hours under control, even when a faster group comes past. Save surges for terrain that demands them and recover afterward.
  2. Protect traction and equipment. Choose a line you can see through; carry the repair items you have practiced using. Check tires, sealant, chain, brakes, lights and navigation before the trip.
  3. Plan each resupply leg. Based on the current edition guide, write down the distance and likely time to the next permitted support point. Carry enough food and fluid for a delay, heat or headwind.
  4. Watch the middle hours. Keep eating and drinking before a deficit feels obvious. If handling, mood or power deteriorate, reset pace and solve the basics before chasing a group.
  5. Finish with decisions, not heroics. Reassess weather, visibility, mechanical state and safe handling. Adjust your goal if conditions change.

Emporia weather and course conditions vary by edition. Check the forecast close to the start, then choose clothing, bottles, tires and pacing for those conditions. Course-specific mile markers and elevation should come from the current organizer release.

7. Final-week warning

Check your applied calendar before racing. The protected Saturday card in this legacy product says “Race Day or Rest (W12Sa)” despite this being a six-week plan. The following Sunday contains a one-hour “5 min Test.” Those labels and placements are unresolved legacy defects; they have not been edited in TrainingPeaks. Align your real race date with the organizer schedule; if your applied calendar places the race on another day, use the actual event date and ignore the mismatched race label. Do not perform a maximal five-minute test the day after completing a 200-mile event. Recover, eat, hydrate, sleep and write a race debrief instead.
